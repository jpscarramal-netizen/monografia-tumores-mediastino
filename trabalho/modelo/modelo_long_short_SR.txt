BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: Longer stent retrievers (SRs) may improve clot integration and increase ﬁrst-pass 
reperfusion in large vessel occlusions. However, their association with higher complication rates, including intracranial 
hemorrhage (ICH), remains uncertain. This study evaluated the impact of SR length on angiographic and clinical 
outcomes in acute ischemic stroke patients undergoing mechanical thrombectomy.
METHODS: A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science to identify studies comparing 
short (≤20 mm) vs long (>20 mm) SRs in mechanical thrombectomy for large vessel occlusion. Efﬁcacy outcomes 
included ﬁrst-pass complete reperfusion, ﬁrst-pass successful reperfusion, ﬁnal complete reperfusion, and ﬁnal successful 
reperfusion. Safety outcomes included good clinical outcome (modiﬁed Rankin Scale 0-2 at 90 days), any ICH, 
symptomatic ICH, subarachnoid hemorrhage, emboli to new territory, vasospasm, and 90-day mortality. Pooled risk 
ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs were calculated under a random-effects model.
RESULTS: Eight retrospective cohort studies (4545 patients; 2280 short SRs, 2265 long SRs) were included. Long SRs 
increased ﬁrst-pass complete reperfusion (RR 1.14; CI 1.02-1.26), ﬁrst-pass successful reperfusion (RR 1.13; CI 1.02-1.24), 
and ﬁnal complete reperfusion (RR 1.09; CI 1.01-1.17). No signiﬁcant differences were observed for ﬁnal successful 
reperfusion (RR 0.95; CI 0.81-1.12), good clinical outcome (RR 0.96; 95% CI 0.88-1.04), any ICH (RR 0.87; 95% CI 0.34-2.21), 
symptomatic ICH (RR 1.11; 95% CI 0.43-2.84), subarachnoid hemorrhage (RR 0.99; 95% CI 0.62-1.56), emboli to new 
territory (RR 0.55; 95% CI 0.16-1.85), vasospasm (RR 0.84; 95% CI 0.50-1.42), or mortality (RR 1.08; 95% CI 0.88-1.32).
CONCLUSION: Longer SRs improve angiographic efﬁcacy, particularly ﬁrst-pass reperfusion, without increasing overall 
complication rates. Their use should be tailored to thrombus burden and vascular anatomy, while future trials must reﬁne 
criteria for optimal SR selection.

The technical goal of MT is to achieve prompt and effective 
reperfusion—deﬁned as a Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction 
(TICI) score of 2b or 3—to maximize the likelihood of a favorable 
functional outcome, ideally within the therapeutic window as 
recommended by current guidelines. 3
Among the endovascular tools used in MT, stent retrievers (SRs) 
have become central to treatment, demonstrating high rates of re-
canalization and good outcomes, particularly when deployed early 
and with optimized techniques, either alone or in combination with 
aspiration. 4 Delivered through a microcatheter and deployed across 
the thrombus, SRs are retrieved together with the microcatheter as a 
single unit, enabling mechanical clot extraction. 5,6 Since the initial 
adoption of SRs in MT, SR design has progressively evolved over 
time, with the introduction of longer and larger SRs, often paralleling 
advances in adjunctive techniques and procedural strategies. 5,6 Al-
though advancements in SR design have improved ﬂexibility and 
compatibility with aspiration systems, speciﬁc technical features such 
as SR length remain underexplored in clinical practice. 5,7
SRs are manufactured in various lengths, typically categorized as 
short (≤20 mm) or long (>20 mm), and selected at the discretion of 
the operator based on anatomic and procedural considerations. 8,9 
It has been hypothesized that longer SRs may enhance clot inte-
gration and increase the likelihood of ﬁrst-pass complete re-
perfusion for LVOs, although this potential beneﬁt may come with 
a greater risk of complications, including intracranial hemorrhage 
(ICH). 10 However, comparative clinical data remain limited, and 
there is no consensus regarding the optimal SR length.
Comparing the clinical outcomes of long vs short SRs in pa-
tients undergoing MT becomes imperative in light of these 
considerations. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and 
meta-analysis to evaluate whether SR length inﬂuences efﬁcacy 
and safety in patients with AIS due to LVO.
METHODS
This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the guidelines 
established by the Cochrane Handbook 11 and the Preferred Reporting Items 
for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement. 12 Registration 
in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; 
CRD420251125492) was completed before conducting the review.
Eligibility Criteria
The included studies met the following criteria being: (1) randomized 
controlled trials and prospective or retrospective cohort studies, (2) 
comparing short (≤20 mm) vs long (>20 mm) SRs for the treatment of 
AIS due to LVO, (3) reporting at least 1 safety and/or efﬁcacy outcome, 
and (4) written in English.
Search Strategy, Data Extraction, and 
Quality Assessment
We performed a comprehensive search on PubMed, Scopus, and Web 
of Science from inception to June 12, 2025, with the following strategy: 
stent AND (retriev* OR stentriev*) AND stroke AND (compar* OR
versus OR vs OR characteristics OR length OR long* OR size OR 
diameter OR caliber). Data extraction and quality assessment were in-
dependently performed by 2 authors, with discrepancies resolved by 
consensus involving 2 additional authors. Risk of bias was evaluated with 
the Risk Of Bias In Non-Randomized Studies Of Interventions. 13 This 
tool evaluates bias across 7 domains, including confounding, selection of 
participants, classiﬁcation of interventions, deviations from intended 
interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of 
the reported result.
Outcomes, Subanalysis, and Deﬁnitions
Clinical and angiographic outcomes were categorized into 2 main 
domains: safety and efﬁcacy.
Efﬁcacy outcomes included: (1) ﬁrst-pass complete reperfusion, (2) 
ﬁrst-pass successful reperfusion, (3) ﬁnal complete reperfusion, and 
(4) ﬁnal successful reperfusion. Reperfusion was assessed using either 
the TICI or the modiﬁed TICI (mTICI) scale, with complete re-
perfusion deﬁned as TICI/mTICI 3 and successful reperfusion de-
ﬁned as TICI/mTICI ≥2b. Final reperfusion was deﬁned as the 
reperfusion status achieved at the end of the procedure, regardless of 
the number of SR passes.
Safety outcomes were: (1) good functional outcome, deﬁned as a mRS 
score of 0 to 2 at 90 days; (2) any ICH within 24 to 36 hours post-
procedure; (3) symptomatic ICH (sICH) within 24-36 hours post-
procedure, deﬁned as any ICH associated with a ≥4 point worsening on 
the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale; (4) subarachnoid hem-
orrhage (SAH); (5) emboli to new territory, considered as thrombo-
embolic events in previously unaffected vascular territories; (6) 
vasospasm; and (7) 90-day all-cause mortality.
Statistical Analysis
We computed pooled risk ratio (RR) with 95% CIs under a 
random-effects model, employing the inverse variance and the re-
stricted maximum likelihood methods. P values were reported in their 
absolute, numeric form with 2 decimal places, and those <0.05 were 
considered statistically signiﬁcant. Heterogeneity was assessed using 
the Q test and its P value, with the magnitude evaluated by I 2 value, 
and I 2 >50% was considered signiﬁcant for heterogeneity. A leave-
one-out analysis was performed to address studies with high hetero-
geneity by systematically excluding 1 study at a time and recalculating 
the pooled results. Statistical analysis and data visualization were 
performed using R 4.3.0 (R Core Team, 2023) with the meta 
package v.7.0-0.
RESULTS
Study Selection
A total of 4030 records were identiﬁed from PubMed (1,154), 
Scopus (1,342), and Web of Science (1,534). After removing 
2832 duplicates, 1198 records were screened by title and abstract. 
Of these, 35 articles were selected for full-text review. After ap-
plying the eligibility criteria, 27 studies were excluded: 21 did not 
compare SR sizes ≤20 mm vs >20 mm, and 6 did not report 
relevant outcomes. Ultimately, 8 studies were included. 8,10,14-19

Baseline Characteristics of Studies and Patients
This meta-analysis included 8 retrospective cohort studies: 4 
were multicenter, and 4 were single-center. In total, 4545 
patients were analyzed, with 2280 treated with short SR re-
trievers and 2265 with long SRs. The mean or median age 
across studies ranged from 66 to 77.6 years. Sex distribution 
was relatively balanced overall, with approximately 964 men 
and 1316 women in the short SR group, and 1195 men and 
1070 women in the long SR group. Baseline National Institutes 
of Health Stroke Scale scores varied across studies, with median 
or mean values ranging from approximately 11.9 to 18.5, 
indicating moderate-to-severe neurological deﬁcits at presen-
tation. A total of 1136 patients (50%) in the short SR group 
and 1113 patients (49%) in the long SR group received in-
travenous thrombolysis.
Occlusions in the middle cerebral artery (MCA) were more 
frequent in the short SR retriever group, with 1975 patients 
(87%), than in the long SR group, with 1625 patients (72%), 
with MCA M1 being the predominant site in both groups (80% 
vs 68%, respectively). Internal carotid artery (ICA) occlusions 
were more common in the long SR group, with 639 patients 
(28%), compared with the short SR group, with 222 patients 
(10%). Posterior circulation occlusions were rare but occurred 
more often in the short SR group, with 83 patients (3.6%), than 
in the long SR group, with 11 patients (0.5%).
Combined procedures were reported in 6 studies, 8,10,14,17-19 
with 1523 patients (79%) in the short SR group and 1775 pa-
tients (83%) in the long SR group. Notably, aspiration was used in 
1537 cases (76%) in the short SR group and in 1324 cases (79%) 
in the long SR group, as reported in 5 studies, 8,10,14,16,18 with 
aspiration-assisted SR thrombectomy during the same procedure

All pooled results from the meta-analyses and subgroup analyses

Efﬁcacy Outcomes
A meta-analysis of 4 studies demonstrated higher rates of ﬁrst-
pass complete reperfusion with long SRs compared with short SRs 
(RR 1.14; 95% CI 1.02-1.26; I 2 = 0%; Figure 2). Besides, long SRs 
were more effective in achieving ﬁrst-pass successful reperfusion 
(RR 1.13; 95% CI 1.02-1.24; I 2 = 35.8%; Figure 3) and ﬁnal 
complete reperfusion (RR 1.09; 95% CI 1.01-1.17; I 2 = 35.8%; 
Figure 4) than short SRs, revealed by pooled data of 7 and 4 studies, 
respectively. However, there was no statistically signiﬁcant differ-
ence between long and short SRs regarding ﬁnal successful re-
perfusion (RR 0.95; 95% CI 0.81-1.12; I 2 = 79.1%; Figure 5). 
Notably, when the study by Styczen et al was excluded in sensitivity 
analysis, heterogeneity was reduced to 0% and the effect estimate 
shifted in favor of long SRs (RR 1.03; 95% CI 1.01-1.04).
Safety Outcomes
There was no statistically signiﬁcant difference between long 
and short SRs regarding good clinical outcomes (RR 0.96; 95% 
CI 0.88-1.04; I 2 = 44.0%), rates of any ICH (RR 0.87; 95% CI 
0.34-2.21; I 2 = 73.4%), rates of sICH (RR 1.11; 95% CI 0.43-
2.84; I 2 = 49.4%) and rates of SAH (RR 0.99; 95% CI 0.62-1.56; 
I 2 = 0%). The analysis of heterogeneity for any ICH identiﬁed 
Ding et al (2024) as the primary contributor to heterogeneity. 
After its exclusion, heterogeneity substantially decreased (I 2 = 
32%), and the pooled effect slightly increased (RR 1.54; 95% CI 
1.35-1.77), indicating a notable inﬂuence on the overall estimate.
Besides, long and short SRs demonstrated no statistically signif-
icant difference in rates of emboli to new territory (RR 0.55; 95% CI
0.16-1.85; I 2 = 44.5%), vasospasm (RR 0.84; 95% CI 0.50-1.42; 
I 2 = 0%), and mortality (RR 1.08; 95% CI 0.88-1.32; I 2 = 0.9%).

DISCUSSION
This meta-analysis, encompassing over 4545 from 8 retro-
spective studies, provides strong evidence that SR length plays a 
critical role in inﬂuencing the procedural efﬁcacy of MT in AIS 
due to LVO. SRs longer than 20 mm—especially those measuring 
30-40 mm—were consistently associated with signiﬁcantly higher 
rates of ﬁrst-pass complete and successful reperfusion, supporting 
the hypothesis that SR could be a modiﬁable factor to optimize 
early revascularization.
The concept of the ﬁrst-pass complete reperfusion has become 
a key procedural benchmark, given its well-established association 
with better functional outcomes, smaller infarcts, and reduced 
health care burden. 20 Our pooled analysis revealed that longer SRs 
were associated with higher ﬁrst-pass reperfusion outcomes rates 
than shorter ones (≤20 mm), particularly within the 4 × 30-

The underlying mechanism is primarily biomechanical: longer 
SRs offer a greater contact surface with the thrombus, improving 
clot integration, reducing fragmentation, and minimizing the risk 
of distal embolization. 19 This is particularly relevant for ﬁbrin-
rich thrombi and occlusions involving long clots, in which clot 
length frequently exceeds 10-15 mm. 21,22
ICA terminus occlusions are typically associated with larger, 
ﬁbrin-rich thrombi, whereas M1 occlusions are more often car-
dioembolic and achieve higher ﬁrst-pass complete reperfusion 
rates. 20,23 In our subgroup analysis of MCA occlusions (84% 
involving the M1 segment), long SRs was associated with higher 
ﬁrst-pass successful reperfusion, whereas no difference was ob-
served in ICA occlusions. However, this analysis was limited to a 
small number of studies reporting location-speciﬁc outcomes, 
underscoring the need for future studies to stratify efﬁcacy and 
safety by occlusion site.
In diameter-based subgroup analyses, long 4-mm SRs were 
associated with higher ﬁrst-pass reperfusion outcomes, whereas no
signiﬁcant difference was observed for 6-mm SRs. However, these 
comparisons are subject to important confounding. In 4-mm 
analyses, both short and long SRs were predominantly used in 
MCA occlusions. By contrast, 6 × 25-40 mm analyses inherently 
compared a higher proportion of ICA occlusions with MCA 
occlusions, which represents a major confounder given the well-
established differences in reperfusion rates between these terri-
tories. 20,23 Notably, in the study by Ding et al, 15 diameter 
comparisons were restricted to ICA occlusions and showed higher 
ﬁrst-pass success and shorter procedural times with a 6 × 30 mm 
RECO SR compared with 4 × 20 mm SRs. However, the in-
dependent effects of SR diameter and length across different 
occlusion sites remain unclear based on available data, and further 
head-to-head comparative studies within the same vascular ter-
ritory are warranted.
Despite higher ﬁrst-pass reperfusion outcomes with longer SRs, 
this technical advantage did not translate into improved func-
tional outcomes or mortality in the present analysis. Stroke 
outcomes may be inﬂuenced by multiple factors—including 
baseline stroke severity, time to reperfusion, thrombus

Importantly, despite the lack of signiﬁcant differences in 
functional outcomes or mortality, the improved ﬁrst-pass re-
perfusion observed with longer SRs was not accompanied by any 
signal of increased procedural or hemorrhagic complications, 
including sICH, SAH, vasospasm, or emboli to new territory. 
This is clinically reassuring, particularly given concerns about the 
potential for longer SRs to induce endothelial injury or hemor-
rhagic transformation. However, subgroup analyses revealed 
subtle differences: the 4 × 30-40 mm group showed a higher rate 
of any ICH, primarily inﬂuenced by the study by Gory et al, 
whereas the 5 × 30-40 mm group showed a lower ICH rate, albeit 
in a smaller sample. 8 These ﬁndings suggest that hemorrhagic risk 
may be more closely related to anatomic context and SR stiffness 
rather than length alone.
Interestingly, in the 6 × 25-40 mm subgroup, a higher 90-day 
mortality was observed. However, this ﬁnding should be in-
terpreted in the context of occlusion site distribution, as 4-mm 
SRs were more frequently used in MCA occlusions, whereas 6-
mm SRs were predominantly used in ICA occlusions—territories 
known to differ in ﬁrst-pass reperfusion rates and baseline severity. 
This difference may also reﬂect larger infarct cores or a higher 
burden of baseline comorbidities in patients selected for large-
diameter SRs. These observations highlight the need for future 
studies directly comparing 4-mm and 6-mm SRs within the same 
vascular territory. Despite variability in SR types (e.g., Solitaire, 
Trevo, RECO, pRESET), efﬁcacy trends remained consistent, 
indicating that length—not platform—is the dominant variable. 
Similarly, adjunctive techniques (e.g., aspiration, balloon guide 
catheter use) and administration of intravenous thrombolysis were 
generally well balanced across studies, minimizing the risk of bias. 
One notable limitation is the heterogeneity in clot burden and 
occlusion site, as most studies focused on anterior circulation 
(ICA/M1), with few including M2 or tandem lesions. As shown 
by Styczen et al, 16 adjusting SR length to thrombus size is essential 
to avoid oversizing in distal occlusions or low-burden cases.
Altogether, our ﬁndings suggest that longer SRs may be ad-
vantageous of longer retrievers (≥30 mm)—speciﬁcally 4 × 40 mm 
and 5 × 40 mm—in anterior circulation strokes with large 
thrombus burden. However, caution is advised with 6 × 40 mm 
SRs, especially in tortuous or fragile vascular anatomy. Future 
prospective randomized studies are needed to validate these 
ﬁndings in homogeneous populations and to establish stan-
dardized, imaging-based SR selection protocols. The integration 
of advanced imaging tools and AI-driven thrombus character-
ization may ultimately enable real-time customization of SR 
choice based on anatomy and clot morphology.
Limitations
This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, all included 
studies were retrospective, introducing potential selection and 
information biases. Second, although most baseline characteristics
and procedural variables were balanced, heterogeneity in occlu-
sion site, intravenous thrombolysis, SR type, and adjunctive 
techniques (e.g., aspiration, balloon guide catheter use) may have 
inﬂuenced the observed associations, reﬂecting the inherent 
limitations of the retrospective observational studies included. In 
addition, the absence of outcomes stratiﬁed by potential con-
founders, combined with the limited number of included studies, 
precluded formal meta-regression to further explore the impact of 
these confounding factors. Third, data were predominantly de-
rived from anterior circulation strokes, limiting generalizability to 
posterior circulation or distal occlusions. Finally, variability in SR 
brands and sizes within the same length category, as well as 
differences in operator experience and center protocols, may have 
acted as uncontrolled confounders.
CONCLUSION
The ﬁndings of this systematic review and meta-analysis suggest 
that SR length may inﬂuence signiﬁcantly impacts procedural efﬁcacy 
in MT for LVO. Longer SRs were associated with increase the 
likelihood of achieving ﬁrst-pass reperfusion, particularly in anterior 
circulation occlusions with high thrombus burden. These beneﬁts are 
not consistently associated with higher complication rates, though 
larger-diameter SRs (6 mm) may carry greater risk in selected cases. A 
tailored, anatomy-guided approach to SR selection is warranted, and 
future trials should aim to reﬁne criteria for optimal SR sizing based 
on thrombus characteristics and vascular anatomy.
